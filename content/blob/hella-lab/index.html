Hella Labs: an agent in the walls

A self-hosted lab where a delegated agent lives inside your infrastructure — and the interesting part is the line it must not cross.

3 servers 3 workstations 23 namespaces 2 trust zones

Most home labs have one principal: you. This one has two.

The terminology here is owner-approved and deliberately precise. "Interactive agent" means an agent running as the human user - your POSIX account, your credentials, your authority. "Delegated agent" means a distinct service principal with bounded authority of its own.

These are authority classes, not product names. A delegated agent is not made interactive by running on a workstation, and an interactive agent is not delegated just because it is remote. The class label grants nothing by itself.

The premise

Most home labs are run by one person.

This one is run by a person and an agent that writes code, merges branches, and operates infrastructure.

Interactive agent

You, with tools

Codex, Claude Code, DSH. Runs as you — your POSIX account, your credentials, your authority. Expensive frontier models, private memory. A wrong turn is your wrong turn.

Delegated agent

Ember, as a service

A distinct service principal with its own bounded authority. Implemented in upstream Hermes. Merges its own work, runs unattended, escalates when it should.

The actual problem

Keeping them apart

Both share hardware, a cluster, a network, and a forge. Separation has to be enforced by machinery, not by good intentions.

One self-hosted Forgejo instance hosts three organizations. qrkourier and bingnet are inside the owner's authority. delegated is outside and is managed by Ember.

The asymmetry is the point. Ember can push branches and open PRs into owner zones, but it cannot merge there - owner approval is the boundary. Inside delegated it is admin, and it merges autonomously.

The delegated PRs often carry a readable history, but that is a byproduct of good practice, not a human gate. Do not let a pretty PR trail convince you the review happened.

The comparison that people usually get wrong.

The temptation is to frame this as "an AI versus a human" or "a cheap model versus an expensive one". It is neither. The difference is authority.

An interactive agent is you. If it deletes the wrong branch, you can undo it because you are the one holding the credentials. Ember is a service principal: if it does the wrong thing, undoing it is a remediation, not a keystroke.

Also note the last row of each column. The reversibility asymmetry is the entire reason the boundary is worth the engineering cost.

Principal comparison

Not a product difference — an authority difference

The class label grants nothing. The POSIX account, forge identity, credential scope and actual enforcement are separate facts — each verified on its own.

hella-nodes/docs/automation-horizons.md · hella-core PLAN.md §"How the trust boundary is expressed and enforced" 07

This is the change-control model, and it is the part most self-hosted labs do not have at all.

Three horizons. The node day-2 lane runs a fixed profile after a protected merge. Flux reconciles ordinary Kubernetes resources. A separate cluster day-2 lane handles credential staging and alert declarations with its own runner identity and its own Vault inputs.

And a human floor: bootstrap, recovery, runner provisioning, and Forgejo failover stay human-executed. Always.

The dispatcher itself is worth pointing at. It is a root-owned entry point that rejects caller-supplied playbooks, limits, tags, inventories, and variables, and verifies a signed Forgejo OIDC token against the repository, workflow, protected ref, and current SHA before Ansible starts. A workflow cannot smuggle in an arbitrary playbook.

delegated/hermes-runtime/docs · hella-core PLAN.md §"Control channels, memory and naming" 09

LiteLLM owns routing for Ember, the workstations, and Open WebUI. It is the single routing owner.

The parent agent elects auto-frontier. Bounded implementation and PR workers elect auto-implementation, which is allowed to start on a budget model and escalate. Everything else - planning, review, diagnosis, synthesis - stays on frontier.

Two rules make this safe rather than merely clever. Risk floors override cost, so a risky task goes frontier regardless of what the classifier thinks. And uncertain classification defaults to frontier rather than to cheap.

The last line of the caption is the one to stress: a routing decision picks a model. It grants no tool-execution authority. Classification is data, and supplied content is data - it cannot talk its way into permissions.

The honesty slide. PLAN.md separates observed, source-declared, and proposed, and the deck carries that distinction deliberately.

Observed means a bounded read-only check with stated limits. Declared means stated in source or config - which does not prove deployment. Proposed means intended policy, not yet built.

The bottom box is the one I would not cut. Two privileged runners are registered organization-scoped rather than repository-scoped, so their scheduling scope is broader than the intended hella-nodes-only boundary. Both lanes currently share one VM, and the two lane Vault password files compare byte-identical, so independent decryption capability is not established. Root inside that shared VM can reach both lanes.

The boundaries are real. They are also not yet independently verifiable, and the owner has an open issue for it. That is a better story than a green checkmark, because it is true.

The closing thought.

A delegated agent is only useful if it can act. It is only safe if it cannot overstep. Those two are in tension, and the whole architecture is the resolution of that tension.

The distinctive part of this lab is not the automation - it is that the boundaries are written down, named, and tracked against the evidence still needed to prove them. Building verifiable edges, and knowing which ones are still unproven, is the actual work.
